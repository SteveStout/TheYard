**A recruiter email that fits your search perfectly is the one to slow down on.**

At 7:50 AM on October 29, 2026, an email titled "Confidential Leadership Search" arrived in my inbox. The role was Lead .NET Engineer, paying $140K to $180K+. It read like it was written off my resume. Honestly, it surprised me how close it came: the title and the level matched my search. I didn't open it.

Email is only one door. My mom got a call that showed up as Charter, with a local Missouri number. A trusted name and a hometown area code. Caller ID can be faked as easily as an email sender's name, so hang up and call back on the number from the company's own website.

Scammers know the market is rough. They know job seekers check email before coffee, and they write the message that person has been waiting for. According to the [Federal Trade Commission](https://search.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2025/09/ftc-takes-steps-stop-deceptive-unfair-labor-market-practices), job scam reports topped 105,000 in 2024, close to triple the 2020 count, and reported losses passed $513 million.

Here are the five checks I run before I reply to an opportunity. The slides below walk the same five, one per slide. Mine failed the first three.

## 1. Check the sender's domain

Retained search firms and in-house recruiters send from the company's own domain. A free inbox costs a scammer nothing and disappears when it gets reported.

In my inbox: the sender was a free Gmail address running a "confidential leadership search."

## 2. Find the thread behind "Re:"

A subject that opens with "Re:" implies an earlier conversation. Search your inbox for it. A fake reply lowers your guard because it looks like a thread you already trust.

In my inbox: no earlier message from that sender existed.

## 3. Treat a locked message as a warning

[Gmail confidential mode](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/7674059) has honest uses, but it also keeps the real content away from spam filters until you click "View the email." A cold recruiter has no reason to hide a job description.

In my inbox: the whole message sat behind that button.

## 4. Find the role on the careers page

Emails and job board listings can be faked. The employer's own careers page is much harder to fake. If the role is not there, ask the recruiter for the requisition number and check again before you send anything.

## 5. Never pay or share your SSN to apply

Background check fees, equipment you buy up front, a crypto deposit to release "earnings" and "direct deposit setup" forms before an offer are the payoff of the scam. A real employer collects your Social Security number after an offer, through its own HR system.

## A simple way to remember

- A free inbox → Not a search firm
- A "Re:" with no thread → A setup
- A locked message → A filter dodge
- Pay or SSN first → Walk away

If something fails, report it. In the Gmail app, open the message, tap the three dots and choose Report phishing. You can also file at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).

Stay vigilant. The email or call you've been waiting on may not be the breakthrough it looks like. A real recruiter will still be there after two minutes of checking.
